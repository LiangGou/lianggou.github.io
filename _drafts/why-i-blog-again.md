## The anxiety I couldn't name

I've been anxious. Not the kind that comes from a bad manager or a failing project — my leadership is supportive, my team is wonderful. This anxiety comes from somewhere deeper: I'm afraid I'm losing my identity.

## A pressure cooker called Silicon Valley

Look at the context. AI is evolving faster than anything I've seen in my career. Big tech is cutting middle management layers. Everyone claims to be doing AI. The whole valley feels like a pressure cooker, and I feel myself getting compressed along with it.

## How I lost my research hat

I'm a researcher by training — IBM Research, Visa Research, Bosch Research — each move chasing wherever AI was booming: social media mining, fraud detection, autonomous driving. Then the LLM inflection point pulled me into production: Splunk, then Cisco. The mission changed from publishing papers to applying state-of-the-art AI for customer value. I enjoyed it. I learned a lot. But my technical identity got buried under enterprise release cycles, and my research hat quietly disappeared.

## Working harder didn't help

So I worked harder. Meetings by day, papers and experiments by night, weekends lost to my own curiosity, trying to inspire my team in between. And still the anxiety grew. My managers kept telling me to rest. I couldn't — because the anxiety was never about workload. It was the fear that what I was working on, what I was thinking about, would become irrelevant to where AI was going.

## A vlogger's advice

Then I came across [课代表立正](https://youtube.com/@kedaibiao), a Chinese tech vlogger, talking about building your own technical influence: don't wait for permission — create a community around your own insights. Start a blog. Share what you actually think. Let the people who value your perspective find you.

So I did. (I built this entire site with Meta Muse — I didn't write a single line of code.)

## The ideas needed a home

That was a few days ago, and something unexpected happened: the anxiety started to lift. Not because the field slowed down — it didn't — but because I started finding value in myself again. I have so many ideas. They were there all along, buried under the pressure. Writing pulls them out.

I'm curious about how things work — I always have been. But at work I can only pursue what serves the business, the product, the team's mission. The questions that keep me up at night, the hunches I get from papers I read after hours — most of them have nowhere to go. Writing here changes that: even if I never implement an idea myself, someone else might pick it up and run with it. That alone makes me happy. And paradoxically, this outlet makes me more motivated at work — I'm relieved of the pressure to cram everything I care about into the boundaries of a job.

## Cheaper and more honest than papers

It's also the cheapest, most honest medium I know. In the labs we'd publish every year — and a paper takes enormous polishing, plus you learn the tricks: the paradigms and routines you follow just to get accepted, which have nothing to do with the actual innovation. Blogging skips all of that. It's the easiest way to record what I'm thinking, share it, maybe influence or inspire someone — and keep a record for myself instead of keeping it all locked inside.

## Discovering who I am

So that's what this blog is: a journey of discovering who I am. Tracking my thoughts, finding where I'm actually good, where my passion really is. I'm blogging again because I need a place where my thinking belongs to me — not to a roadmap, not to a release cycle. I'm not chasing a platform or an audience. I just want to write things down here — and if you find something useful in them, I'll be super happy.
